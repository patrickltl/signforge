The complete guide to HTML email signature generators

What actually works in Gmail, Outlook and Apple Mail — and why most "email signature generators" produce signatures that fall apart.

What an HTML email signature generator actually does

An HTML email signature generator turns a form full of details — your name, title, company, phone numbers and links — into a small block of HTML that your email client appends to every message. The catch is that email is not a browser. Gmail, Outlook and Apple Mail each strip, rewrite or ignore parts of modern HTML, so a signature that looks perfect in Chrome can arrive as a pile of misaligned text in a colleague's Outlook. A good generator therefore does two jobs at once: it makes the signature look good, and it writes HTML that survives the trip through real email clients.

SignForge does the second job seriously. Every template is built from nested <table> elements with inline styles only — no external CSS, no flexbox, no grid, no JavaScript — because that is the subset of HTML that every client from Gmail to twenty-year-old Outlook builds correctly. Images always use absolute https:// URLs, and fonts fall back to web-safe stacks like Arial. That is unglamorous engineering, and it is exactly why the signatures hold their shape.

Using an email signature generator with Gmail

If you searched for an email signature generator for Gmail, here is what matters. Gmail stores signatures in your account settings, not as a file, so installation is a copy-paste into the signature editor: open Gmail on desktop, click the gear, choose See all settings, and scroll to the Signature section on the General tab. Create a new signature, click into the box, and paste — a rich-text paste carries the formatted HTML across, which is why SignForge's copy button copies formatted content rather than raw code.

Two Gmail quirks are worth knowing. First, Gmail caps signatures at 10,000 characters; SignForge shows a live counter under the preview so you never hit the limit silently. Second, Gmail on mobile can be set to use the same signature as desktop, but check the "signature defaults" toggles for new emails versus replies. The full, click-by-click walkthrough is in the Install guides modal inside the builder.

Outlook is why you need a different kind of generator

People searching for an Outlook email signature generator usually got burned before: they built a beautiful signature, pasted it into Outlook, and watched the layout collapse. The reason is that classic Outlook on Windows renders email through Microsoft Word's HTML engine, which ignores huge parts of modern CSS — flexbox, grid, background images, max-width and custom fonts all behave badly or vanish.

SignForge's templates are designed around Word's limitations rather than fighting them. Layouts use table cells for structure, spacing comes from cell padding instead of margins, and color arrives through inline styles that Word respects. Rounded photo corners quietly become squares in classic Outlook — a graceful degradation, not a broken signature. The Install guides modal covers Outlook on Windows, Mac and the web separately, because each has its own settings path.

Why table-based HTML wins

There is a reason email designers still say "tables and inline styles" like a mantra. Webmail clients sanitize your HTML: Gmail removes <style> blocks and some attributes, Outlook rewrites spacing, Apple Mail reflows at different window widths. Table rows and columns with explicit widths and inline styles are the one layout system every client agrees on. When you download a signature from SignForge, open the .html file and view its source — you will see clean, hand-shaped table markup you can host, edit or reuse anywhere, with no dependencies.

Images: hosting, URLs and why pastes break

Headshots, logos and banners must be hosted somewhere the recipient's client can reach — a URL that starts with https://. Embedded (base64) images are blocked by most clients, and images attached to the email never survive the signature format. Good free options for hosting: your own website's media library, a CDN, or any reputable image host. Keep images small (a headshot around 200×200 pixels is plenty), because every recipient downloads them. If a signature ever shows a broken-image box, the first thing to check is whether the URL is public — open it in an incognito window to test.

Dark mode, contrast and accessibility

In dark mode, clients treat signatures differently: Gmail and Apple Mail may dim or partially invert colors, while classic Outlook often leaves them untouched. Nobody can promise pixel-identical dark-mode rendering — any generator claiming otherwise is guessing. What you can control is contrast. SignForge's palettes pair dark ink text with bright accents, so a dimmed signature still reads clearly. Send yourself a test email and view it with dark mode on before you roll a signature out to a team.

Install your signature

Press "Copy signature" first, then follow the steps for your client. The formatted HTML comes across with a normal paste.

Gmail (web)
  1. Open Gmail on your computer, click the gear icon (top right) and choose See all settings.
  2. Stay on the General tab and scroll down to the Signature section.
  3. Click Create new and give it a name (e.g. "Main").
  4. Click Copy signature in SignForge, click inside the Gmail signature box, and paste (Ctrl+V / Cmd+V).
  5. Below the editor, set the new signature under For new emails use and On reply/forward use.
  6. Scroll to the bottom and click Save Changes.

Tip: Gmail caps signatures at 10,000 characters. The counter under the SignForge preview shows you're well within it.

Outlook (Windows, classic desktop)
  1. Open Outlook and go to File > Options > Mail.
  2. Click the Signatures… button.
  3. Click New, name the signature, then click inside the Edit signature box.
  4. Press Copy signature in SignForge and paste into the box (Ctrl+V).
  5. Under Choose default signature, assign it to your account for new messages and replies/forwards.
  6. Click OK, then OK again to finish.

Tip: Outlook for Windows renders with Word's engine — SignForge templates are already built for that, so paste as-is and don't re-style it.

Outlook on the web
  1. Click the gear icon, then Mail > Compose and reply.
  2. Under Email signature, click into the editor and paste (Ctrl+V / Cmd+V).
  3. Choose whether to add it automatically to new messages and replies.
  4. Click Save.
Outlook (Mac)
  1. Open Outlook and go to the Outlook menu > Preferences (or Settings) > Signatures.
  2. Select your account and click + to add a new signature.
  3. If Always match my default message font is checked, uncheck it first — otherwise your formatting gets stripped.
  4. Click inside the editor and paste (Cmd+V).
  5. Set it as default under Default signatures, then close the window.
Apple Mail (macOS)
  1. Open Mail and go to Mail > Settings (called Preferences on older macOS) > Signatures.
  2. Select your email account in the middle column, then click + to create a signature.
  3. Uncheck Always match my default font — this is the one checkbox that strips your formatting.
  4. Click inside the signature editor on the right, then paste (Cmd+V).
  5. Drag the signature in the middle column to set the default order for new messages vs replies.

Tip: if the paste looks plain, click into the editor again and paste once more — Mail occasionally needs a second paste when the editor wasn't focused.
